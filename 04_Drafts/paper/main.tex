\section{Introduction}
Real-time rendering must balance image quality against the time available to
produce each frame. Rendering at a lower resolution and reconstructing a
higher-resolution image can reduce rendering work, but the reconstruction
itself consumes time and must deal with aliased or missing detail
\cite{xiao2020neural,mercier2023efficient}. Temporal reconstruction reuses
samples from previous frames, while neural methods can combine learned image
features with information supplied by the renderer. Both approaches must
handle motion and avoid introducing visible artifacts.

Rendering buffers offer geometric and temporal cues beyond color. Prior
work shows how these cues can assist reconstruction: Xiao et al.\ combine
depth and motion-aligned history in a neural system, while Wen et al.\ study
rendering inputs as part of an architecture-optimization framework
\cite{xiao2020neural,wen2025cgsr}. Mercier et al.\ compare temporal
reconstruction with and without warping and show why image-wide scores can
miss thin-object artifacts \cite{mercier2023efficient}. These findings
identify useful inputs and evaluation concerns, but their effects depend on
the model, rendering setup, and processing used for each comparison.

We propose a controlled study of auxiliary inputs for a lightweight neural
upscaler using Mercier et al.'s QRISP dataset
\cite{mercier2023efficient}. Our primary comparisons isolate depth and
motion-alignment effects on matched current-frame and single-history
inputs, with reconstruction quality, runtime, memory, and scene-level
variation evaluated under one protocol. Surface-normal comparisons remain
conditional because QRISP does not document a surface-normal modality.
The paired comparisons estimate how depth changes the benefit of
alignment in this model.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Efficient Neural Reconstruction}
Real-time reconstruction must recover detail within a frame-time budget.
Xiao et al.\ show that a neural supersampler can use renderer-provided depth
and motion vectors to combine current color with reprojected features from
previous frames \cite{xiao2020neural}. Its architecture and combined input
changes, however, do not isolate the effect of each cue. Mercier et
al.\ develop a more efficient temporal network and a rendered gaming dataset;
their model-size and component studies connect reconstruction quality to
inference cost and the workload being measured \cite{mercier2023efficient}.
Wen et al.\ instead integrate rendering properties into network construction,
architecture search, and pruning across super-resolution backbones
\cite{wen2025cgsr}. Their optimization demonstrates that input choice and
model computation can change together. These approaches motivate studying
input choice in relation to the cost of reconstruction, rather than treating
quality differences as properties of the inputs alone.

\subsection{Geometric Guidance}
Depth indicates scene distance, while surface normals indicate orientation;
they may therefore supply different cues at edges and on changing surfaces.
Wen et al.\ sequentially add depth, normal direction, and derived edge
information in CGSR, relating those properties to reconstruction errors and
model size \cite{wen2025cgsr}. This is an existing rendering-input ablation,
but its sequential design and network optimization couple input choice to
changes in capacity.

Zheng et al.\ place normals and material information in a detail-reconstruction
module, while depth and motion constrain temporal feature refinement
\cite{zheng2025rdg}. Their ablations compare decoupled guidance against
concatenation and alternative fusion modules. They show that the way signals
are combined matters, rather than isolating each buffer's contribution.
Together, these studies motivate examining depth and normals individually
and together while distinguishing input choice from fusion design.

\subsection{Temporal History, Alignment, and Failure Cases}
Temporal history can supply samples missing from the current frame, provided
they correspond to the current view. Karis's temporal antialiasing method
uses motion vectors to reproject history and constrains incompatible history
with a current-frame neighborhood \cite{karis2014high}. It also describes
failure at silhouettes and where motion is inaccurate. These principles
inform our interest in aligned history without implying adoption of the
complete antialiasing method.

Xiao et al.\ compare single-frame and temporal reconstruction, and study
alternative reprojection settings \cite{xiao2020neural}. Their separate
depth-and-motion-input comparison also replaces estimated optical flow with
renderer motion vectors, so it does not isolate either change. Mercier et
al.\ explicitly compare their temporal model with no warping and with
estimated flow \cite{mercier2023efficient}. Their results do not determine
the effect in a different lightweight model and rendering setup.

Both works also caution against relying only on whole-image averages. Xiao
et al.\ show disocclusion ghosting when feature reweighting is removed
\cite{xiao2020neural}. In Mercier et al.'s 2$\times$ medium-model ablation,
removing depth-informed motion-vector dilation improves aggregate PSNR and
LPIPS, although their thin-object example favors dilation
\cite{mercier2023efficient}. Such cases make localized artifacts relevant to
interpreting reconstruction quality, even when aggregate scores improve.

Taken together, these studies motivate examining renderer-provided geometry
and temporal history in relation to both reconstruction quality and
computational cost. Prior work already includes input and alignment
ablations, but their effects depend on model design, fusion, and rendering
conditions. Our study aims to clarify these trade-offs in a selected
lightweight model and rendering setup.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
We ask whether current-frame depth helps a small upscaler, whether one
previous RGB frame helps, and whether the benefit of aligning that frame
depends on access to depth. We propose the following protocol for an
offline experiment on Unity-rendered sequences. Training and evaluation
are pending.

\subsection{Data and scene split}
We will pair QRISP's non-jittered $960\times540$ RGB render (mipmap bias
$-1$) with the same frame's $1920\times1080$ Enhanced target. This target
comes from a 2160p render with eight-sample MSAA. The matched 540p depth
and motion buffers supply the auxiliary inputs
\cite[Supplement, Annex~A]{mercier2023efficient}.
We will use stored RGB code values divided by 255, discard color alpha,
and apply no further exposure or transfer function. For depth, the four
unsigned PNG bytes give
$D=R/255+G/255^2+B/255^3+A/255^4$.
We will check the decoded range on training captures and use the
documented near-to-far encoding directly. The 1080p target contributes
only to supervision and scoring.

QRISP has ten development scenes and three published test scenes. We
propose holding the entire SciFiBase environment family for validation:
SciFiBase, ScifiBaseStartStop, and ScifiBaseNightStartStop. This retains
moving and stop-containing captures in validation and keeps alternate
views of that environment together. The other seven development scenes
supply training data. This grouping separates environments, although
individual assets can recur across splits. Validation selects checkpoints;
the official test scenes (AbandonedSchool, SpaceShipDemo, and Seaport)
enter only the final evaluation.

The sampling unit is a current frame with its consecutive predecessor
in the same segment. All models, including the single-frame baselines,
use the same eligible current frames for training and scoring. Removing
one initial frame per segment gives expected counts of 4,640 training,
2,378 validation, and 1,495 test pairs from the published segment lengths
\cite[Supplement, Table~5]{mercier2023efficient}. We will reconcile these
counts with a manifest of scene, segment, frame, and modality paths,
record gaps or exclusions, and keep all captures of each frame in one split.

\subsection{Inputs and comparisons}
Every model receives current RGB. Table~\ref{tab:variants} crosses the
geometry available at the current frame with three ways to use the
previous rendered LR RGB frame. Only that earlier RGB image is warped.
Aligned models use the motion field during preprocessing. The CNN never
receives raw motion or previous depth. All primary captures have jitter
off, and all models have an empty recurrent state.

Let $q_i$ denote the scene-balanced test PSNR in dB for variant $i$,
averaged over matched seeds. Our directional expectations are that
depth improves single-frame reconstruction ($q_1-q_0>0$) and alignment
improves temporal reconstruction at each depth setting ($q_5-q_4>0$
and $q_7-q_6>0$). We will estimate the change in alignment benefit
$(q_7-q_6)-(q_5-q_4)$ without prespecifying its sign. The contrasts
$q_4-q_0$ and $q_6-q_1$ measure adding unaligned history. Depth gains
with unaligned and aligned history are $q_6-q_4$ and $q_7-q_5$.
These comparisons concern this model and this quality scale.

QRISP documents no independent normal buffer. A normal map reconstructed
from depth would test a transformation of depth. If a second capture
source provides normals, we would run both the normal variants and
their controls on that same source. If jitter is studied, a small
secondary comparison would retrain V4 and V5 on their matched jittered
captures with the same mipmap bias, targets, splits, and seeds. The CNN
would receive no offset channels in either condition. We would use the
recorded offsets to verify the warp and correct for relative jitter only
if the supplied vectors omit it. Comparing the two alignment gains then
tests alignment under different sampling conditions. Learned recurrent
features would require a separate stateful model and training protocol.

\subsection{Model and alignment}
Each variant has ten LR input channels in the same order (RGB, depth,
three reserved normals, previous RGB). Missing inputs are zero. A
$3\times3$ convolution maps ten channels to 32, followed by ReLU. Four
residual blocks each add the output of two $3\times3$, 32-channel
convolutions to their input, with ReLU between the convolutions. A final
$3\times3$ convolution produces 12 channels. A $2\times$ pixel shuffle
rearranges them into an HR RGB residual, which we add to bilinearly
upsampled current RGB (half-pixel centers). All convolutions have bias,
unit stride, and one-pixel zero padding. There is no normalization layer.
Each model has 80,364 trainable parameters. Input slots held at zero
contribute no signal even though the dense network has the same size.

For aligned history, let $d_t(p)$ be displacement from current LR pixel
center $p$ to its source in the preceding image. We bilinearly sample
the previous RGB at $p+d_t(p)$ over the full frame before taking a
training crop. QRISP stores vertical then horizontal velocity. Decoding
requires scaling by height and width, respectively, and reconciling
Unity's vertical axis with image rows
\cite[Supplement, Annex~A]{mercier2023efficient}.
On training data we will verify the backward mapping against identifiable
moving points, and check identity and known one-pixel translations on a
synthetic grid. We will record the tested conversion. Sampling uses
half-pixel centers and border replication. We use the published motion
field without depth dilation, an occlusion mask, or a learned confidence
channel. Unaligned variants read the original previous RGB at $p$.
Disoccluded regions and overlays remain in the quality scores, including
UI elements that lack corresponding motion or depth.

\subsection{Training and checkpoint selection}
For each seed (17, 29, 43), all six models start from the same initial
weights and train independently. We draw an eligible training pair
uniformly with replacement, then draw an integer crop origin uniformly
over valid $64\times64$ LR patches. The HR crop starts at twice that
origin and spans $128\times128$ pixels. The seed fixes the same list of
pairs and crops for every variant. We use cropping alone for augmentation.

We propose 20,000 updates per run, batch size eight, and mean absolute
RGB error on the unclipped prediction. AdamW uses constant learning rate
$10^{-3}$, betas $(0.9,0.999)$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, and weight decay
0.01. Every 1,000 updates we score full eligible validation frames and
keep the checkpoint with the highest equal-scene mean PSNR, choosing the
earlier checkpoint on a tie. The six variants and three seeds require
18 runs (360,000 updates). Before committing to this budget, we will
pilot V0 and V7 in FP32 on the target 8-GB RTX~4060, record peak memory
and end-to-end updates per second, and project the total time including
validation. Any adjustment to precision, batch size, or update count will
apply to all runs and be recorded before the comparison begins. We will
save split and crop manifests, source checksums, seeds, package versions,
Git revision, checkpoints, and validation curves.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:metrics}
We will score full frames in the stored RGB encoding, including image
borders, after clipping predictions to $[0,1]$. The primary metric is
$10\log_{10}(1/\mathrm{MSE})$ using one MSE over all RGB values in
each frame. We average frame PSNRs within each scene, then weight the
three test scenes equally. We will show each scene's paired differences
for the three matched seeds and report the mean and range across seeds.
The three scenes constrain claims about other environments. Bicubic
interpolation of current RGB provides a non-neural reference using the
same pixel-center convention and scoring rules. Secondary SSIM uses
an $11\times11$ Gaussian window ($\sigma=1.5$), range one, standard
constants $K_1=0.01$, $K_2=0.03$, population covariance and valid windows,
averaged over channels \cite{wang2004ssim}.

To describe temporal behavior, we will also report the mean absolute
difference between successive output changes and reference changes in
screen coordinates, computed on evaluation RGB:
$\left|(\hat I_t-\hat I_{t-1})-(I_t-I_{t-1})\right|$.
This requires two consecutive scored frames in the same segment. We will
use the same scene weighting and inspect synchronized reference and
output clips for moving silhouettes, newly visible regions, and UI.
This statistic measures error in frame changes and can respond to
ordinary reconstruction errors on moving edges. We will interpret it
alongside spatial quality and clips.

We will time batch-one, full-frame inference at $1920\times1080$ output
in evaluation mode on the same GPU and precision. With decoded buffers
resident on device, we will report network-only time and time from
separate buffers to clipped HR output, including input assembly and
any motion conversion and warp. We will use CUDA events, 50 warm-up
frames and 200 timed frames from a fixed sequence, synchronize before
reading the events, and report median and 95th percentile. We will
reset peak allocated-memory statistics after warm-up and report peak
allocated GPU memory for each pipeline in isolation. Disk I/O, host
transfers, and scene rendering are outside both timings. These measurements
characterize offline reconstruction cost. An engine frame rate requires
timing the integrated renderer.
