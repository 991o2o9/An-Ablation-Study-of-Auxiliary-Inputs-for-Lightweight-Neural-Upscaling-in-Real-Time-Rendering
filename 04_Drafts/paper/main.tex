\section{Introduction}
Real-time rendering must balance image quality against the time available to
produce each frame. Rendering at a lower resolution and reconstructing a
higher-resolution image can reduce rendering work, but the reconstruction
itself consumes time and must deal with aliased or missing detail
\cite{xiao2020neural,mercier2023efficient}. Temporal reconstruction reuses
samples from previous frames, while neural methods can combine learned image
features with information supplied by the renderer. Both approaches must
handle motion and avoid introducing visible artifacts.

Rendering buffers offer geometric and temporal cues beyond color. Prior
work has examined their use and ablated components: Wen et al.\ investigate
rendering properties including depth and normals in a framework that also
optimizes network size, and Mercier et al.\ compare temporal reconstruction
with and without warping \cite{wen2025cgsr,mercier2023efficient}. These
studies motivate, but do not determine, the effects of selected inputs in a
different model and rendering setup. The quality of a reconstruction also
cannot be assessed solely from an aggregate score: for example, Mercier
et al.\ report a benefit of depth-informed motion processing on thin objects
even when removing that processing improves aggregate scores in their test
\cite{mercier2023efficient}.

We plan a controlled study of auxiliary inputs for a lightweight neural
upscaler. Under a shared experimental protocol, we intend to compare depth
and normals individually and together, then compare unaligned with
motion-aligned history and examine the joint addition of depth and normals
to aligned history. We will assess reconstruction quality alongside measured
runtime and memory costs, variation across held-out scenes, and spatial
failure cases. This is a proposed comparison within a specific setup, not a
claim that input or alignment ablations are new. The architecture, data, and
measurement boundaries remain to be fixed before experiments.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Efficient Neural Reconstruction}
Neural reconstruction can exploit rendering information as well as color, but
a more capable model must fit within the rendering time budget. Xiao et al.\
combine low-resolution color and depth with motion-aligned features from
previous frames in a temporal neural supersampling system
\cite{xiao2020neural}. Mercier et al.\ develop an efficient temporal
architecture and a gaming dataset with rendered depth and motion; their
model-size comparisons illustrate that quality and inference time depend on
the selected network and workload \cite{mercier2023efficient}. Wen et al.\
take a different approach, using rendering information to guide network
construction, architecture search, and pruning across super-resolution
backbones \cite{wen2025cgsr}. Thus, comparing their results requires
considering both the supplied information and changes in computation. A
latency reported for one method does not by itself establish the total cost
of another pipeline, including buffer preparation and temporal processing.

\subsection{Geometric Guidance}
Depth and normals describe different aspects of scene geometry and can be
used to guide reconstruction. Wen et al.\ investigate the sequential
addition of rendering properties, including depth, normal direction, and
object edges derived from depth, in their CGSR framework
\cite{wen2025cgsr}. Their analysis relates reconstruction errors to geometric
regions and examines accuracy and model size; it also changes the network
through its optimization framework. This informs our planned single-frame comparisons of color alone, depth,
normals, and their combination, but does not by itself establish their
effects in our proposed shared architecture.

Geometric signals can also enter different parts of a temporal model. Zheng
et al.\ use normals together with material information to guide detail
reconstruction, while depth and motion guide temporal feature refinement
\cite{zheng2025rdg}. Their comparison of decoupled guidance with other fusion
designs tests how information is incorporated rather than isolating the
effect of each buffer. These approaches motivate comparing individual and
combined geometric inputs, while reporting changes in model capacity and
cost alongside quality. In particular, adding depth and normals together to aligned history cannot
identify their separate effects in the temporal setting.

\subsection{Temporal History, Alignment, and Failure Cases}
Temporal reconstruction uses motion to relate samples across frames. In
Karis's temporal antialiasing method, motion vectors reproject history and
neighborhood clipping limits incompatible history
\cite{karis2014high}. Neural supersampling likewise uses motion-based
alignment, but must handle disocclusions and changes that surface motion
does not capture, such as changes in lighting \cite{xiao2020neural}.
Xiao et al.\ compare a single-frame model with their temporal model; in a
separate comparison they add depth while replacing estimated optical flow
with renderer motion vectors. The latter changes both inputs and alignment
mechanism, so it does not isolate either effect.

Mercier et al.\ also compare their temporal method with a no-warp variant,
and test depth-informed motion-vector dilation \cite{mercier2023efficient}.
In their 2$\times$ medium-model ablation, removing dilation improves
aggregate PSNR and LPIPS, whereas their visual comparison shows reduced
artifacts on thin objects with dilation. Xiao et al.\ illustrate ghosting
at disocclusions when their feature-reweighting module is removed
\cite{xiao2020neural}. These observations motivate examining localized
failures as well as aggregate quality; they do not establish that any
planned variant will improve those regions. Our proposed comparison of unaligned and motion-aligned history will test
alignment within the selected model, rather than the independent effect of
motion vectors or the complete temporal methods above.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Study Design and Analysis Topics}
We plan to study a color-only single-frame baseline (V0) and three spatial
variants that add depth (V1), normals (V2), or both (V3). A planned temporal
extension adds unaligned history to color (V4), aligns that history using
motion vectors (V5), and adds depth and normals together to aligned history
(V6). Table~\ref{tab:variants} summarizes the proposed inputs. The temporal
model and the representation of history remain to be selected.

\paragraph{Individual and combined geometric inputs.}
V1 versus V0 estimates the effect of depth, and V2 versus V0 estimates the
effect of normals, subject to the capacity and processing controls below.
V3 versus V1 estimates the incremental effect of normals with depth, while
V3 versus V2 estimates the incremental effect of depth with normals.
Together, these comparisons can examine overlap between their contributions
in the single-frame setting.

\paragraph{Temporal history and alignment.}
V4 versus V0 examines adding unaligned history, with any differences in
temporal processing taken into account. V5 versus V4 tests motion alignment
provided that history and temporal processing are otherwise matched. V6
versus V5 tests adding depth and normals together to aligned history; V6
versus V3 compares the combined temporal and single-frame variants. These
comparisons do not isolate the separate effects of depth and normals with
aligned history, motion vectors without history, or every interaction among
the inputs.

\paragraph{Quality, cost, and generalization.}
We plan to compare reconstruction quality with the selected runtime and
memory measurements. Training, validation, and test scenes will be disjoint
at the scene level; test frames will not be used for fitting or protocol
selection. We will examine whether variant differences persist across
held-out scenes and how they vary among them. An unseen scene does not by
itself establish a distribution shift.

\paragraph{Spatial benefits and failures.}
Alongside aggregate quality, we plan to inspect where variants help or hurt,
including geometric edges, thin structures, and regions newly revealed by
motion. Error maps and selected image regions can expose local effects that
may be obscured in whole-image averages.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
The following are expectations to be assessed after experiments; they may be
supported or refuted.
\begin{description}
\item[H1] Adding depth improves quality over color-only input.
\item[H2] Adding surface normals improves quality over color-only input.
\item[H3] Depth and normals are partly redundant, so using both improves
quality by less than the sum of their individual gains.
\item[H4] History warped with motion vectors improves quality over history
used without alignment.
\item[H5] Improvements of selected auxiliary-input variants over their
matched baselines persist on scene-disjoint test scenes.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Controls and interpretation.}
We plan to use a shared base architecture while varying inputs. A shared
design does not imply identical parameter counts: input channel changes
affect the first layer, and temporal variants may require additional
processing. Training data, loss, optimizer, and schedule should be held
constant where applicable. We plan multiple random seeds, with the number to
be determined. We will report parameter counts and computational complexity,
and control capacity where feasible, for example by matching parameter
budgets. Any remaining differences in capacity or processing will be
acknowledged when interpreting quality and cost. The architecture, temporal
implementation, and final protocol remain to be fixed.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:metrics}
The following evaluation choices are provisional until the image pipeline and
protocol are fixed. No measurements have yet been made. We propose PSNR and
SSIM for reference-image reconstruction: PSNR summarizes pixel error, while
SSIM compares local image structure \cite{wang2004ssim}. Neither establishes
perceptual quality or temporal stability on its own.

For spatial analysis, FLIP is a candidate for perceptually motivated
reference-image error maps \cite{andersson2020flip}. If evaluation uses
low-dynamic-range (LDR) images, LDR-FLIP applies to the chosen display-ready
images. If the study evaluates high-dynamic-range (HDR) images, HDR-FLIP is a
distinct method that combines maps over exposure-compensated, tone-mapped
image pairs \cite{andersson2021hdrflip}. The choice depends on the final
pipeline. FLIP maps indicate estimated visible differences; they do not
prove human preferences. LPIPS is an optional complementary learned-feature
distance \cite{zhang2018lpips}, rather than a required metric: its additional
value for these rendered images should be established before inclusion, and
its score does not establish human preference. Framewise FLIP and LPIPS do
not establish temporal stability.

We will select representative crops and sequences illustrating geometric
edges, thin structures, and disocclusions, including cases where inputs help
or hurt. Such examples are descriptive. Any systematic regional comparison
requires independently defined masks, a selection rule fixed before test
analysis, and reporting the number and coverage of regions; if reliable
masks cannot be defined, we will not treat selected crops as regional
statistics. Metric maps near mask boundaries may depend on context outside
the region.

Temporal stability requires a separate sequence-level assessment. As a
provisional quantitative option, we may examine temporal variation on
verified static segments, alongside the reference sequence; Mercier et al.\
use pixel-wise temporal standard deviation on a static scene segment
\cite{mercier2023efficient}. This can reveal flicker in that restricted
setting but does not characterize moving content, and a stable blurred output
may score well. We also plan synchronized reference/output video inspection
for moving scenes, including disocclusions, using predeclared selection and
playback conditions. A motion-compensated temporal error may be considered
if reliable correspondences and visibility masks are available: its motion
source, warping and exclusions must be fixed independently of the variants.
Warping errors, occlusion, lighting changes, and blur can confound the result;
excluded pixels and coverage must be reported. Neither this measure nor
selected videos alone would establish general temporal perceptual quality.

Before testing, the protocol must specify implementation and version for
each measure; reference alignment, frame synchronization, resolution and
cropping; color space, transfer function, range, exposure and tone mapping;
and the treatment of invalid pixels. For PSNR, specify peak value, channels,
and whether aggregation precedes or follows the logarithm; for SSIM, specify
window, scale, channels and range. For FLIP, specify the LDR or HDR variant,
display and viewing assumptions, map pooling, and, for HDR, exposure and
tone-mapping settings. If LPIPS is used, specify its backbone, weights, input
normalization and pooling. For temporal assessment, specify static-segment
criteria, frame rate, duration, reset handling and any correspondence and
masking procedure. Fix aggregation across pixels, frames, sequences, scenes,
and seeds; report per-scene results and the weighting used for summaries.
These choices must apply consistently to the variants.

Possible deployment costs include neural inference latency and GPU memory
consumption, as well as buffer preparation, history warping and storage,
data transfers, and total upscaling-pipeline time. The protocol must define
measurement boundaries, hardware, timing procedure and unmeasured costs.
Evaluation metric computation, reference processing, and offline inspection
are separate from deployment latency and must not be included in it unless
explicitly identified as a separate measurement. The measurement protocol
and any interpolation baseline remain to be fixed before the experiments.
